# Watching movies with focus on the cinematography 
Every summer break, I spend a week on the seaside,
and because I am not the best beach-goer, I watch as many movies as my emotional
and intellectual capacities can digest. This summer, I decided to make
a selection of movies that are not well-known to the general public, but are
widely recognized by people in the filmmaking industry. I decided to watch the
movies differently to how I usually do - not for their plots, or to judge them
based on my subjective overall impression, but to focus my attention to the
shooting decisions and acquaint myself with different filming styles. I ended up
watching movies by six different directors, listing the movies together with
their director below:

**Sergei Urusevsky** - *I Am Cuba / Soy Cuba* 
**Kazuo Miyagawa** - *Kagi / Odd Obsession* 
**Gabriel Figueroa** - *The Night of the Iguana* 
**Sven Nykvist** - *Persona* 
**Christopher Doyle** - *Fallen Angels*
**Jack Cardiff** - *Black Narcissus* 

Below I share some of my observations and thoughts, for my recollection of
my impressions of these movies. All of them were incredible, and the work their
authors put into making them is undeniably impressive and creatively rich. By
sharing my thoughts, I do not want in any way to detract from or add anything to
these two facts. If you want to take a jab at these movies, please refrain from
reading below and experience them yourself! You will not regret it.

### 1. Sergei Urusevsky — the centerpiece *(USSR)* 

"I Am Cuba" is an absolute titan of cinematographic mastery. A black-and-white
movie about the life of people in a tyrannical regime, the camerework of this
movie makes it what it is.  Urusevsky uses all kinds of different shots
- intimate hand-held face close ups, grandoise crane shots and soaring mid-air 
angles to convey a huge range of emotions in this movie. Being trained as an
artist, his mastery of dynamism is on display with his camerawork.

From the starting shot, the viewer knows they are in for a ride. A single
long-shot on a rooftop in Havana, the camera moves through a crowd of people
having a party, afterwards descending on a makeshift elavator setup before
transitioning back to a handheld shot, plunging inside a pool. This shot is one
of the most well-known, but it is just the start. Afterwards, the viewer is
taken to a bar, where a singer moves through the bar serenading women and
entertaining the bar-goers. The movement through the bar transitions between
surveying the scene, make uses of dutch angles and close-ups, 
completely capturing the viewer's attention, zooming between the singer
and the individual women, sipping their drinks and smoking their long cigarette
holders. The dance scene, where one of the women half-willingly dances with an
American 'client' was also something I had never seen in a modern movie, for all
of the technological and technical advances we have in the movie industry, which
is a real shame, given how absolutely in-place and affecting it was. The closest
analogue I have seen in a modern movie is the Monn Mothma dance scene in Andor,
but even there the cinmetographer did not dare display movement in the dramatic
and mesmerizing way Urusevsky does.

The visual effects were all technically creative. For example, when one of the
characters gets shot, the shadow-world of smoke and water intermingling,
absolutely stayed with me even a month after the movie. Another incredibly
affecting scenes was a farmer's rage after being indignantly and unceremoniously
dispossed of his own land. In the shot the camera tracks the violent 
movement of his hand, making the viewer experience the frustration, anger and
rage.

Though a variety of techniques, Urusevsky mangages to capture an insane amount
of emotions in a single movie, such as euphoria, shame, anger, sadness, elation,
reverance and defiance. Just having a couple of these in a movie is impressive,
but such a range is absolutely a must-see for anyone who is curious about what can
be achieved through camera-work.
